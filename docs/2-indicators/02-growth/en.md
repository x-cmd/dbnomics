# Economic growth: the decade matters more than the year

Two economies can both grow 'about 2%' and mean opposite things. One is a rich country inching along because its population is old and its machines are new; the other is a country still moving factories inland and wiring up a grid. The number on the news is the same. The decade behind it is not.

Here is the same six economies since 2000, and the picture is mostly about the 2008 crash and the 2020 pandemic: the sharp vertical drops in 2009 and 2020, the violent rebounds after each, and in between the two stories everybody argues about. China runs the highest line for most of the period and then stops — a straight, visible deceleration rather than a crisis. The United States and the euro area hug the bottom, with the euro area's 2020 collapse (−6.1%) the deepest of the lot. Japan never leaves the basement. Average annual growth, decade by decade, and the ranking keeps changing hands. In the 2000s China averaged 10.3% a year, India 6.3%, Brazil 3.4% — and the United States, at 1.9%, was already slower than Brazil. In the 2010s China slowed to 7.7% while India held 6.6% and the gap closed. In the 2020s so far India is the fastest of the six at 5.4%, ahead of China's 4.7%, with the euro area down at 1.0%. A generation of commentary said China's peak was 2010; on these numbers its peak decade was the one before, and the country overtaking it on average growth is no longer a forecast.

Where the idea came from is worth a minute, because 'growth' is younger than it sounds. In 1956 Robert Solow showed that in the long run a country's output per worker depends on how much capital it has and how productive it is — everything else is a detour. The postwar Golden Age (1950–73) then gave the West something to forget: growth near 4% for twenty-five years, an oil shock and a productivity slowdown later killed it, and the word 'stagflation' was coined to describe the two at once. China then supplied the biggest growth episode ever recorded, roughly 10% a year for a quarter-century, and the 'catch-up' logic behind it — economies starting lower grow faster because there is more distance to cover — is what the first chart is really showing.

**Takeaway.** Growth is a rate, and a rate only means something next to a starting point and a time window. Three things survive from this page: the poor and middle decades go to whoever is catching up, not to whoever is largest; the 2009 and 2020 drops are shocks, not the trend (the rebounds are flattered by a collapsed base); and China's own trend has halved twice, which is normal for a catch-up economy, not a sign of collapse. When someone tells you a country is 'growing fast', ask fast compared with which decade, and against whom.

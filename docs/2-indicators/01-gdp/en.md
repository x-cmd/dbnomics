# GDP: how big an economy is, and the yardstick that changes the answer

Ask how big a country is and there is one number everybody uses: the GDP, the value of everything its people made in a year. It is worth roughly $30 trillion for America and $19 trillion for China — and those two figures alone decide most arguments about who is winning. The trouble starts with the second question: big compared to what, measured how?

The G20 is the club of the world's largest economies — nineteen countries (the US, China, Germany, Japan, India, the UK, France, Italy, Brazil, Russia, Korea, Canada, Australia, Mexico, Indonesia, Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Argentina) plus the EU and the African Union. It is a forum for cooperation, not a market and not a voting body. Rank them by this year's GDP and there is a cliff: America and China together are close to half the club, and then the third place, Germany, arrives with less than a sixth of the leader's size. Averages are for the people who sell averages.

Now change the yardstick and the ranking changes with it. The second chart prices the same output at what it actually costs to buy inside each country — the PPP yardstick, and on it China is not second but first, at $39 trillion of 'international dollars' against America's $30 trillion. India climbs from fifth to third. Nothing about the economies moved; only the ruler did. That is the whole argument about whether China has overtaken the United States, and why the answer depends on which ruler you pick.

Grouped into blocs the same trick is louder. On PPP the BRICS — Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa — come out at about $70 trillion against the G7's $59 trillion; at market exchange rates the G7 still leads, $52 trillion to $29 trillion. ASEAN, the ten Southeast Asian economies, is small in dollars and roughly triples in PPP terms — the next bloc to watch, and the one the market still prices at a discount.

Where the number came from is stranger than most people expect. GDP was invented for an emergency. In the mid-1930s, with the New Deal and then the war looming, the US government needed to know how big the economy was and how fast it was growing; Simon Kuznets built the first national income accounts for Congress in 1937, and the United Nations turned the template into a global standard in 1944. The name itself is newer than most people think: the statistics office called the measure GNP until 1991, when it renamed it *gross domestic product* and retired a category that had counted Americans' foreign earnings and left out their foreign taxes. The number that runs the world is a wartime statistic that became a religion.

And this is the sentence you have heard: China has overtaken America. On the PPP ruler it is simply true — the red line crosses the blue in 2016 and by 2025 China is 30% larger in the goods its people can buy. On the market ruler, which is what you have to borrow in and what foreign investors get paid in, China is still at 64% of American output, and the gap has barely moved in a decade. Same country, two rulers, two headlines. Which one you quote tells the reader what you want them to conclude, and nothing about the economy itself.

**Takeaway.** Size is a fact; ranking is an argument. The GDP number is not in dispute — only what you compare it with. So whenever you meet a claim about who is bigger, richer or winning, ask two questions: which ruler, and which date. On today's data America is still the largest economy at market rates, China is the largest at purchasing power, and the G20 as a whole is more uneven than its members like to admit.

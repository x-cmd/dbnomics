# Unemployment: the fastest number, the slowest story

There is one economic figure most people check before they check anything else, and it is the one most likely to be wrong on the evening news: unemployment. It is also the fastest-moving number in the economy — and the part of it that never moves.

Eight economies over the last quarter-century. Two things stand out. First, the 2009 and 2020 spikes are enormous and brief: America goes from 5.0% to 9.6% and back to 3.6% in six years, so the labour market can undo a decade of damage in a cycle. Second, the lines that do not move are the interesting ones: the euro area sits an order of magnitude above the United States almost the whole time, and Japan sits an order of magnitude below everyone. Those gaps are not the cycle. They are institutions.

This is the American number since 1948, the longest run available. The peaks are a museum: 7.9% after the Korean War, 9.5% in the mid-1970s, 10.8% in 1982 (the hardest American recession since the war, and the price of killing inflation), 9.6% in 2010, 8.1% in the pandemic. And between the peaks the floor has been falling — from 5.5% in the 1950s to 3.5% before 2019 — which is what a country does when more women, more older workers and a better safety net meet an economy that keeps adding jobs.

The number itself is younger than you think, and its definition was fought over. Unemployment was measured informally long before it was measured officially; the modern rate dates from the Current Population Survey, which the US Census Bureau started in 1948, and the internationally comparable definition — people without work who actively looked for it in the past four weeks — comes from the International Labour Organization, standardised in 1933. John Maynard Keynes made unemployment the centre of economic theory in 1936; a generation later Milton Friedman gave the concept its other half, the *natural rate*: the unemployment that would exist even if nobody tried very hard. Everything about the current debate — how much slack is left, whether a 4% rate means a weak economy — is an argument about where that line sits.

**Takeaway.** Unemployment is the number that moves fastest and the gap that closes slowest. Three things survive from this page. First, the cyclical part of unemployment reverses: 2009's scars were gone within six years, while the gap between the euro area and the United States has not moved in thirty. Second, that gap is made of things monetary policy cannot touch — how expensive it is to fire someone, how school-to-work works, whether a second job exists. Third, the rate you are quoted is a fraction of a number that includes a judgement: in most countries the official rate covers urban workers and leaves out the people hardest to count, which is why the official Chinese rate has sat near 5% for years while graduate unemployment tells a different story.

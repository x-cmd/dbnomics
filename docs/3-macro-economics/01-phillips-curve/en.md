# Phillips curve: the inflation–unemployment trade-off

Your grocery bill and your job are connected, and economists have known it for a century. Why does the same basket of milk, rent and petrol cost more in some months and less in others? And why does the price of a haircut go up exactly when the number of people looking for work goes down? The Phillips curve is the oldest policy rule in macroeconomics: inflation and unemployment move in opposite directions, so a government can pick which one to have more of. Each line here is one country's path from 2000 to 2025 — x = inflation, y = unemployment — not a fitted curve.

**Judgment**: America drew the smoothest loop anyone has seen in 40 years. The line dives into the 2020 pandemic pit, where inflation was low and unemployment was high. It climbs to the 2021–23 overheating stretch, when inflation hit 8% while unemployment stayed under 4%. Then it comes back down: in 2024 inflation was 3.0% and unemployment 4.1%. Rate hikes killed the inflation without a recession. China sits in the low-inflation corner at about 0.4% — not the curve working, just weak demand. The rule is not a law of nature. It breaks when the shock comes from supply rather than demand, and that is exactly what separates the US path from Europe's.

The euro area is the third line. The 2021–23 energy shock pushed inflation to an 8.4% peak while unemployment kept falling to 6.5%. Same two numbers, opposite story: a supply shock lifts prices without creating the jobs that would justify them.

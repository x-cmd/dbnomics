# The Phillips curve: the trade-off that keeps breaking

In 1958 a New Zealand-born economist named A.W. Phillips published a paper with a forgettable title and an unforgettable picture. He had a century of British data — 1861 to 1957 — and he plotted unemployment against wage growth. The dots fell along a smooth curve: when people were out of work, wages barely rose; when jobs were plentiful, wages raced ahead. Every finance minister since has wanted to believe him, and most of them have been wrong at least once.

**What the curve says.** The Phillips curve is the oldest trade-off in macroeconomics: inflation and unemployment pull in opposite directions, so a government can pick which one it wants more of. Each line in the first chart is one country's path from 2000 to 2025 — x = inflation, y = unemployment — not a fitted curve. It is the closest thing economics has to a menu: *choose a point on the curve and you have chosen your unemployment rate and your inflation rate at the same time*.

**Reading the paths.** The US line traces a complete loop: the 2020 pandemic pit (1.3% inflation, 8.1% unemployment) → the 2021–23 move to 8.0% inflation with unemployment down at 3.6% → 2025 back to 1.9% inflation and 4.4% unemployment. Rate hikes killed the inflation without a recession: the smoothest ride in forty years. China sits in the low-inflation corner (0.2% in 2023) — not a triumph of the curve, just weak demand. The euro area is the third line and behaves differently: its 2021–23 energy shock pushed inflation to 8.4% while unemployment kept falling to 6.5%, the shape of a supply shock rather than a demand one.

**Where the idea came from.** Phillips was not a statistician and not a Keynesian — he was an engineer from New Zealand who had worked in the oil industry and then, at the London School of Economics, spent his evenings counting. His 1958 paper in *Economica* used a century of British unemployment and wage data, and the relationship was so stable it looked like a law of nature. Two things happened next. First, the trade-off was renamed and redrawn: Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow called it the Phillips curve at the 1959 American Economic Association meeting and, in 1960, swapped *price* inflation onto the vertical axis where Phillips had put wage growth — turning a description of the labour market into a menu for policy. Second, it was used. The Kennedy and Johnson administrations ran America straight down the curve, squeezing unemployment from 5.2% in 1961 to 3.5% in 1969 and letting inflation climb from 1% to nearly 6%. That deal looked like free money for sixteen years.

**The trade-off in one picture.** Here is the same relationship for America since 1961, computed from the Bureau of Labor Statistics: annual inflation (red) against the unemployment rate (blue). Through the 1960s the two lines are mirrors — unemployment falls from 6.7% in 1961 to 4.5% in 1965 while inflation climbs from 1.1% to 5.9% by 1970. Then they stop behaving. The 1970s bring 11.0% inflation in 1974 *and* 8.5% unemployment in 1975; the decade opens with 13.5% inflation in 1980; the 1990s are tame on both counts; and 2009 is the mirror image again, with inflation at −0.3% while unemployment hits 9.3%.

**Case 1 · the decade the curve died.** The chart below puts a number on what happened. The correlation between US inflation and unemployment runs **−0.62** across the 1961–70 decade — the trade-off, working exactly as advertised — and then **+0.22** across 1971–80. The sign flipped. The usual suspects are the two oil shocks (1973 and 1979), a wave of falling productivity, and — in most economists' view — people changing their minds. Milton Friedman had already argued in 1968 that there is no permanent trade-off, only a temporary one: if everyone expects 6% inflation and you deliver 8%, you get a little extra output for a while, but once expectations catch up the extra output disappears and only the extra inflation is left. Edmund Phelps made the same argument independently. Then came the bill. Paul Volcker raised rates brutally from 1979 and drove unemployment to 9.7% in 1982 to get inflation back down to the ground.

**Case 2 · 2021–23, when both went the wrong way.** The pandemic produced something the textbook did not predict: America got high inflation *and* record-low unemployment at the same time. In 2022 inflation ran at 8.0% while unemployment sat at 3.6% — the top-right corner of the scatter below, a place the old curve said was empty. The cause is a supply shock landing on top of a demand shock: broken supply chains, an energy squeeze, and a fiscal hand-out that parked money in people's accounts. You cannot squeeze inflation out of a supply shock without squeezing output as well — unless you push prices back down with demand, which is exactly what the Fed did, and why the euro area, whose shock was pure energy, ended up with the same inflation and a much worse growth picture.

**Takeaway.** The Phillips curve is not a law; it is a snapshot of a world in which inflation is unanchored and shocks come from the demand side. Three things survive from 1958. First, inflation and unemployment usually pull in opposite directions, so they cannot both run hot for long. Second, the more firmly people expect a number, the flatter the curve gets — expectations, not the labour market, are what moved the line in 2001–10 and again in 2020–25. Third, and most dangerous, a supply shock can push both up at once: that is the stagflation of the 1970s and the pandemic of 2021, and it is why central banks talk about inflation expectations rather than about a menu. The honest summary is that a central bank can choose the inflation it tolerates; what it cannot choose is the unemployment that comes with it.

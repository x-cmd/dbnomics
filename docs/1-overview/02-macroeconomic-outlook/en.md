# The 2025 macro world, on one page

By the end of 2025 the United States, the euro area and China had given the same answers to the three questions that decide how a household budget feels: prices back at target, growth positive, nobody in a recession. That agreement is the interesting part, because underneath it the three are running three completely different economies. Here is the year in five charts, and then a table to take it further.

Growth first, because it sets the scene. The 2020 collapse was the deepest since the war — America fell 2.2%, the euro area 6.1% — while China still grew 2.2% and carried the world through it. The rebound of 2021 was violent everywhere (China +8.4%, America +6.1%, the euro area +6.2%), and then three years of settling down. Where they settled is the story: **China 4.5%, America 2.2%, the euro area 1.2%**. The euro area spent 2023 and 2024 growing less than 1%, technically a recovery and practically a stall, while China halved its own pace without falling over. Read the three lines together and the question stops being who is winning and starts being who is running out of runway.

The inflation scare ended, and it ended almost everywhere at once. In 2022 America hit 8.0% and the euro area 8.4% — the euro area's number pushed up by imported energy, America's by a labour market that would not stop hiring — while China sat at 2.0% with the virus still shut. By 2023 the roles had swapped: Chinese consumer prices were up just 0.2%, and the deflation scare of late 2023 was really a scare about weak demand rather than an economy sliding into Japanese-style decline. In 2025 all three are back in the same neighbourhood, **1.9% in America, 2.0% in the euro area, 1.7% in China**, which is the most welcome macro news of the decade and also the most disorienting: the price level is roughly a quarter higher everywhere than in 2020, and nobody feels richer for it.

One story worth watching closely is China's quiet rebalancing. The gap between what China saves and what it invests *is* its current account. In 2007 China was saving 50.4% of GDP and investing 40.4%, and the ten-point difference was the trade surplus the rest of the world complained about. By 2025 saving had fallen to 43.4% while investment rose to 41.8%, leaving a 1.6-point surplus, one of the lowest in decades. The reason is demography: a shrinking working-age population saves less for its old age, and households have started building that buffer themselves. It is the biggest quiet shift in the world economy, and these two lines are all you need to watch it.

**Takeaway.** 2025 was the year the three big economies agreed on the headlines and disagreed on everything underneath. Prices are at target everywhere, so the easy questions are settled. The same year shows America running the world's largest deficit and debt with the second-fastest growth, China running the smallest surplus with a debt ratio that has quadrupled, and the euro area running the smallest deficit with growth barely above 1%. Three economies, three balance sheets, one shared belief that this is about to normalise. The table below is where each of those stories opens up.

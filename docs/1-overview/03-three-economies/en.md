# The US, Europe and China: three economies, three arguments

Everybody has an opinion about which of the three big economies is winning. The trouble is that "winning" means a different thing for every question you ask — and the charts answer differently every time. Put the United States, the euro area (the twenty EU members that use the euro) and China on the same dials, and the argument stops being about slogans: it becomes about whose job market is tight, whose shopping basket is calm, who is still growing, and who is borrowing the most.

**First: the job.** The euro area entered this century with 9.1% of its people out of work — more than double America's 4.0% — and by 2013 it had reached 12.1%, the worst point in the series. America, meanwhile, spent 2019 at 3.7%: about as tight a labour market as any developed economy has ever seen. China's official rate reads 5.1% — but look at the line before you quote it: it is a survey of urban workers, the series only starts in 2017, and it says nothing about migrant workers or the graduates who cannot find a job. The gap between Europe and America is real; the number that puts China in the middle is the softest of the three.

**Second: the shopping basket.** The inflation wave of 2021–23 hit America (8.0% in 2022) and Europe (8.4%) hard and barely touched China (2.0%). By 2023 China was the odd one out, but for the opposite reason: with consumer prices up just 0.2%, its problem was not inflation but its absence, and the "deflation" scare of 2023–24 was really a scare about weak demand. All three are near target now — 1.9% in America, 2.0% in the euro area, 1.7% in China.

**Fourth: the bill.** America is the outlier: 124% of GDP in 2025, up from 53% in 2001, having briefly touched 132% in 2020. China's government debt has quadrupled — 23% to 94% — and now sits above the euro area's 88%. That is the single most argued-over number in the Chinese case, because it depends on whether you count the off-budget financing vehicles local governments lean on. So who is winning? By jobs, America. By prices, nobody — all three are at target. By growth, China. By debt, the euro area. That is why the argument never ends: each side keeps picking the dial that flatters it. The useful thing about putting the three economies on one page is that you can finally see which dial you were choosing all along.

**The same four arguments in one shape.** A radar throws the four dials onto four axes. Every axis is scaled between the best and the worst of the three economies — 100 is the leader on that dial, 0 the laggard — and every axis runs in the direction where more is better: less unemployment, closer to the 2% target, faster growth, less debt. Read the shapes, not the areas. America is a spike on jobs and a hole on fiscal space: unemployment of 4.4% against the euro area's 6.4%, debt of 124% against the euro area's 88%. The euro area is a flat line across the middle — champion on prices and on fiscal space, last on jobs and on growth, which is what "no drama and no growth" looks like from above. China is the mirror image: full marks on growth, 4.9% a year over 2023-25 against America's 2.6% and the euro area's 0.8%; near zero on prices, since 1.7% inflation sits further from the 2% target than either of the others; and quietly respectable on jobs (5.1%) and debt (94%). Each economy is champion on one dial and loser on another, which is exactly why no scoreboard can settle the argument.

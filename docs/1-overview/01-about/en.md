# Economics you can see

Twenty-three pages, every one of them charts, and every chart is a link: click it for the picture, read it for the numbers. Start here anyway. Six shots from the series, the ones most likely to change your mind. Ten minutes on this page is the whole argument; the rest of the site is the receipts.

Who is winning? Nobody, it turns out — it depends which question you ask. America, the euro area and China on the same dials: by jobs America wins (the euro area opened this century with 9.1% unemployment against America's 4.0%, and still sits two points above it), by growth China wins (4.5% against 2.2% and 1.2% in 2025), by debt the euro area wins — though China has quietly quadrupled its own ratio in a quarter-century, from 23% of GDP to 94%. Can a country have high inflation *and* a full labour market? It can, until it can't. In 2022 America ran 8.0% inflation with 3.6% unemployment — the corner of the chart where the old textbooks said nobody could go. What makes it worth drawing is what came after: the trade-off was measurable in 1961–70 (correlation −0.62), it had vanished by the 1970s (+0.22), and it came back in 2001–10 (−0.77). Expectations, not the labour market, decide which world you are standing in. [→ The Phillips curve](../../3-macro-economics/01-phillips-curve/en.md)

Fifty economies, one credit card. Washington runs one fiscal policy, one Fed map and one bankruptcy court for states that share a currency and almost nothing else. New York earns $116,900 a head; Mississippi $53,800. That is a factor of 2.2, and 24 years of *convergence* did not close it: in real terms Mississippi grew 1.2% a year against New York's 1.8%. x dbnomics data imf.us.gdp.growth         # one series
```sh
x dbnomics align imf.us.gdp imf.cn.gdp    # multi-series, aligned
x dbnomics alias                           # preset alias table
```

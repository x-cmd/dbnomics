# China provinces: one country, thirty-one economies

Your home province decides where you can work, what your children can study, how much land you may buy, and what your factory pays. It also runs its own schools, its own courts and a large part of its own tax bill. Underneath all of it sits one currency, one industrial policy and one rail network — and thirty-one provincial economies that are more different from each other than most of them are from entire countries. The OECD's regional database puts them side by side with the American states on exactly the same definition, so this page is a mirror of the last one: five charts, same five questions.

First the income per head. Beijing is on top at $31,700 and Shanghai just behind at $30,200; Gansu is at the bottom with $7,350. The spread was 11.7 times in 2000 and is 4.3 times now — the narrowest it has been since the reform era, and the clearest sign that the coast is no longer the whole story. But hold that number next to the American one: Mississippi, the poorest US state, earns $53,800 a head, more than Beijing. Not one Chinese province is as rich per person as the poorest American state. And the gap *inside* China is now twice as wide as the gap inside America. Convergence with the rest of the world is one thing; convergence inside China is another.

So who grew? The scatter puts each province's income per head in 2000 against its real growth since, and this time the poor really did catch up: the correlation between where a province started and how fast it grew is −0.67. Guizhou managed 10.4% a year over twenty-three years, Shaanxi 9.7%, Anhui 9.6%, Chongqing 9.5%; Guangdong, the coastal giant, managed 5.7%, Tianjin 5.6%, Shanghai 5.1%. Catch-up, though, is not arrival. The heatmap shows when the catching up happened, and the answer is: in waves, each with its own winners. In 2005-10, the stimulus years, the west and the centre ran at 14% a year — Ningxia 14.6%, Shaanxi 14.6%, Hubei 14.2%, Guizhou 14.1% — while Shanghai managed 4.4% and Beijing 5.0%: the coast was the laggard, for the first time in the reform era. In 2015-20 the map flips again, this time against the north: Tibet 8.3% and Yunnan 7.8% at one end, Liaoning 2.7% and Shandong 2.9% at the other. And in the last three years the coal belt is back on top — Shanxi 10.8% a year, Inner Mongolia 10.3%, Xinjiang 8.9% — while Guangdong manages 4.3%. Twenty-three years, three different winners, and the same man-made coastline of industry deciding each time which province gets its turn.

And the people moved with it. Since 2000 Guangdong has added 55.1 million people, more than any European country has today; Zhejiang 21.9 million, Jiangsu 13.1 million, Shandong 12.0 million, Shanghai 10.1 million and Beijing 9.3 million. On the other side Heilongjiang has lost 7.6 million, Jilin 3.4 million, Sichuan 1.9 million, Anhui 1.1 million and Hubei 1.0 million. China moved hundreds of millions of people from the interior to the coast and then, more recently, back into the interior — the migration ledger of the largest reorganisation of labour in human history.

One word on the numbers, because they are younger than the American ones. Chinese provincial GDP is measured the same way as every other region in the OECD database, but the series only reach back to 1997 and the levels have been revised more than once — the 2019 clean-up of provincial fiscal data alone moved several provinces by several per cent. That is why the level charts stop at 2024 and the growth chart stops at 2023: the last year for which the constant-price series is complete. **Takeaway.** Thirty-one economies under one currency. The income gap inside China has halved in twenty years and the poorest provinces have grown four times faster than the richest ones, which is real convergence and worth celebrating. But look at the treemap and then at the heatmap: a third of the economy sits in three coastal provinces, the wave moves every decade — inland, then north, then the coal belt — and the people follow the jobs rather than the averages. The averages say China is catching up with the world. The heatmap says which provinces are doing the catching up, and for how long.

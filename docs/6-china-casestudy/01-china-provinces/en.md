# China provinces: the coastal gap

Think of your home province. Its wages, its factory jobs, its tax rates. Now put it next to the coast: one currency, one industrial policy, one rail network, 31 provincial economies. The OECD's regional database lets us look inside: GDP per capita in 2024, real growth since 1999, and 24 years of migration. The numbers are in current US dollars, so every province faces the same exchange rate — this compares provinces with each other, not countries with countries.

**Judgment**: the gap is wide but it narrowed — 11.7x between Shanghai ($3,910) and Guizhou ($335) in 2000, 4.3x between Beijing ($31,723) and Gansu ($7,350) in 2024. Convergence in growth is real: the correlation between where a province started and how fast it grew over 1999–2023 is −0.65, with the inland west (Guizhou 10.2% a year, Shaanxi 9.7%) growing far faster than the coastal giants (Guangdong 5.9%, Shanghai 5.3%). But the coast grew in size too, so the level gap narrowed without closing. The real divergence is people. Guangdong has 55 million more people than in 2000 — more than Poland's entire population today — while Heilongjiang lost 7.6 million and Jilin 3.4 million. Economic convergence runs north-west; demographic divergence runs south-east. The two pull the map apart.
